# The six lenses

Each lens asks one question of the pages and answers it with quotes. Work through them in order: each one feeds the next, and the last feeds the headline options.

## The fact list

Before judging anything, list what the pages and brand memory actually say, one fact per line with its source: `F3 · about.html · "We don't serve retail, e-commerce or franchises with more than 15 locations."` Brand-memory facts keep their tag: use `confirmed` as fact, flag `provisional`, never state `assumed`. Every finding, every fix and every headline option draws only on this list. A fact that isn't on it doesn't exist for this report, however likely it seems.

## 1. Who it's for, and who it's not

- **Question:** could a buyer tell in ten seconds whether this is for them?
- **Look for:** the audience in plain terms (who, what size, where), exclusions ("not for", "we don't serve"), and where each appears. An audience named only on the about page is a finding even when it's sharp.
- **Weak signs:** "teams", "businesses", "modern brands", or an audience that changes between pages (merchants on one page, agencies on another).
- **When pages conflict:** don't pick one silently. If brand memory names the audience, recommend that one and say so. If not, it's the company's call: name both with quotes, and in guided mode ask.

## 2. Category frame

- **Question:** what shelf would a buyer put this on after reading the headline?
- **Look for:** a category noun a buyer would search for ("restaurant bookkeeping", "scheduling software for dental clinics") against a benefit or a slogan with no category.
- **Weak signs:** "platform", "solution", "the X for modern Y", or two frames that pull against each other (an "AI-powered" banner on a service sold as "a real person").

## 3. Problem and the alternative

- **Question:** what would the buyer do instead, and does the site say why this is better than that?
- **Look for:** the problem in the buyer's words, and the alternative the site names or implies: a spreadsheet, a general provider, a person doing it by hand, doing nothing.
- **Never supply the alternative from memory.** If the site doesn't name one, say so. You may suggest likely alternatives as questions to confirm with customers ("Do prospects compare you with general bookkeepers or with doing it in-house?"), labelled as assumptions, never as findings.

## 4. Differentiated value and proof

- **Question:** what's true of this company that the alternative can't claim, and where's the evidence?
- **Look for:** a specific mechanism or result (how it works, a measured outcome), then the proof beside it: a named customer, a number with its source, a case study.
- **Weak signs:** proof that doesn't match the claim (a testimonial about prime cost under a headline about speed), unattributed numbers ("500+ brands"), and superlatives with nothing behind them.
- A claim's own page can't prove it. Proof is a named customer, a measured result, or a substantiated line in brand memory.

## 5. Consistency across pages

- **Question:** do the home, pricing and about pages describe the same product, for the same buyer, with the same core benefit?
- **Build a table:** page, headline, audience, core benefit, CTA label. Differences in the CTA label are noted but belong to conversion.
- **Watch for:** the about or how-it-works page saying it better than the home page; pricing written for a different buyer; a capability claim on one page that another page contradicts.

## 6. Specificity and the swap test

- **Question:** which claims could a competitor also make?
- **Specificity check (always):** go through the headline, subhead and the main benefit lines. For each, could any company in this category say it unchanged? Mark each specific or generic, quoting it.
- **Swap test (only with supplied competitor pages):** quote each shared or near-identical phrase from both sides with the page names. Then name the site's claims that no supplied competitor page makes. Those are the raw material for the headline.
- **No competitor pages:** say the named swap test wasn't run and what would let you run it. Never name competitors or describe them from memory, and never describe what "competitors usually say".
- Competitor claims stay theirs: "Trusted by 2,000 sales teams" is their claim, not a fact.

## Headline options

- Two or three options, each a headline (about 12 words or fewer) and a subhead (about 30 words or fewer), plus one line saying which facts it's built from (fact IDs or quotes with pages).
- **Built only from the fact list.** No new numbers, customers, capabilities or results. Use the site's own figures exactly.
- **No unsupported claim stated as fact, in any wording.** If the report found a claim unsupported, no option states it or a restatement of it as fact ("ready by the 3rd" is the 3-day claim again). If an option needs it, mark that option "needs proof first" and say what proof, and don't recommend it.
- **Follow the audited company's brand memory:** its rules, its never-use words and its spelling. With no brand memory, follow the site's own spelling convention, not yours.
- **No comparative claims about a named competitor** ("better than X", "faster than X") unless the competitor's pages are supplied and brand memory holds a substantiated version of the claim. Otherwise offer the strongest true, specific angle instead.
- **If P1 already scores 20, keep the headline.** Say so in the verdict. Options become optional tests, labelled "optional test, not a fix", and the plan never puts a headline change in the next 48 hours or 2 weeks (a test can go in the 3-month horizon). A sharper subhead is not a reason to rewrite a headline that already does its job.
- **Lead with what's the company's own.** With competitor pages, the recommended option leads with a claim no competitor page makes (the swap test's result), not with the category or the audience alone. Without them, lead with the most specific true line the specificity check found.

## Maturity

Classify with evidence, using exactly one of the full audit's four stage names: founder-led (under about 10 people, no marketing role), first hire (about 10–50 people, one marketer or hiring the first), small team (2–5 marketers), full function. A team of 14 hiring its first marketing coordinator is "first hire", not "small team". It doesn't change the points. It changes the plan: a founder-led company gets one sharp headline change and one proof line, not a messaging program.
