# Writing check: does this read as generated?

Run this on every draft after the checker passes. These are the tells a script can't catch. A failure means rewrite the sentence, not swap a word.

Work in this order. Fixing word choice in a message that says nothing produces well-dressed emptiness.

## 1. Is anything actually being claimed?

- **Specificity.** A first-touch email should contain at least one named thing (a company, product, person or place), one number, or one consequence the reader would recognise. A message with none of these has no content, and polishing it is cosmetic. Either get the missing fact from the user or cut the message down to what's real.
- **The swap test.** Swap in a competitor or a different company. If the sentence still works, delete it or make it specific.
- **The deletion test.** Cut the sentence. If the message loses nothing, leave it cut.
- **Never invent a specific to make the text feel grounded**, whether a number, a customer, a date or an anecdote. Fabricated detail reads beautifully, and it's a lie. A marked hypothetical ("say a 40-person team") is honest. "A 40-person team we worked with" is not, unless one exists.

## 2. Borrowed rhetorical moves

- **Significance clauses.** An "-ing" clause announcing why a fact matters ("…, highlighting the need for…"). Cut the clause and keep the fact.
- **"Not X, it's Y" / "not just X, but Y."** Say Y.
- **Groups of three.** Three adjectives, three benefits, three examples. Use one or two.
- **Vague authority.** "Studies show", "teams are finding", "experts agree". Name the source or drop the claim.
- **Hedging.** "Might potentially help". Commit or cut.

## 3. Shape

- Three sentences of similar length in a row. Vary them: a four-word sentence next to a twenty-word one.
- Every paragraph built the same way (claim, elaboration, transition). Outreach rarely needs more than two or three short paragraphs.
- Transitions doing no work: "Additionally", "Furthermore", "That said". Delete them.

## 4. Email and DM register

Outreach is transactional writing, so tighten for it:
- No markdown: no bold, headers or bullet lists. A bulleted cold email is a brochure.
- No greeting theatre ("I hope this finds you well") and no sign-off theatre ("Looking forward to hearing from you!").
- Use contractions. Their absence reads as machine-written.
- No exclamation marks in a first touch.
- At most one em dash per message, and usually none. Use commas, colons or full stops.

## 5. Stock phrases

Swapping words is the last 5% of the fix. These are the clusters that still give drafts away:
- Openers: "I hope this email finds you well", "I came across", "I wanted to reach out", "quick question".
- Closers: "worth a chat", "open to a quick call", "let me know your thoughts", "would love to connect".
- Filler: "streamline", "unlock", "leverage", "seamless", "game-changer", "in today's fast-paced", "navigate the complexities", "elevate", "robust", "cutting-edge".
- Follow-ups: "just following up", "circling back", "bumping this", "touching base", "did you get a chance".

## Don't overcorrect

Bad de-slopping produces its own tells. Undo any of these:
- Everything in clipped fragments. Uniformly short sentences are still uniform.
- Performed candour: "Honestly?", "Look,", "Here's the thing".
- Manufactured contrarianism: "Everyone tells you X. They're wrong."
- Fake-casual hedges: "like 40-ish percent". Either you have the number or you don't.
- Rhetorical questions as connective tissue: "So what does that mean?"

The final test: **would the seller say this out loud to this one reader without performing?** If the sentence needs an audience to work, rewrite it.
