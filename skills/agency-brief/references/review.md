# Reviewing a proposal or a first draft

## What to score

Score against the brief, not against what a good proposal looks like in general. Each row gets a result and a one-line note.

| Area | Question | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Deliverables | Is each numbered deliverable in the brief clearly in the proposal, with the specs and quantity? | "6 of 6" or "4 of 6: missing #3, #6" |
| Mandatories | Do the samples, approach and terms respect the brand rules, the claims list, the legal lines, accessibility and ownership? Do sample ads fit the platform's limits (Google search ads: headlines 30 characters, descriptions 90)? Count, don't estimate | OK, or each breach quoted with its length |
| Claims | Is every claim the agency makes about itself or about the company's results supported or verifiable? Does sample copy use only approved claims? | OK, or each claim quoted |
| Budget | Total cost against the brief, with every fee, markup and setup charge added up | "$2,850 vs $3,000 cap: fits" |
| Timeline | Start, key dates, review rounds and delivery against the brief | Fits, or the slip in days |
| Terms | Length, renewal, notice, cancellation, payment terms against the brief | Fits, or the conflict |
| Response questions | Did they answer what section 12 asked? | Answered, or which are missing |

## Red flags

Each red flag in the reply: the quoted line, why it matters in one sentence, and what to ask for instead.

| Red flag | Why it matters | Ask for |
|---|---|---|
| Guaranteed rankings, traffic, leads or ROAS ("#1 in 30 days", "first page guaranteed") | Nobody controls search rankings or auctions; the guarantee usually means vanity keywords or an exit clause | A plan, the metrics they'll report, and estimates labelled as estimates |
| Agency owns or creates the accounts, keeps the data, or gives you read-only access | You lose your history, audiences and campaigns when you leave | Accounts in your name with you as owner; agency gets user access; everything stays yours after |
| Asking for passwords or logins by email | Anyone with the password can lock you out | Grant user access through the platform's own settings |
| Long lock-in: 12-month minimums, auto-renewal, cancellation fees, large non-refundable setup fees, when the brief asked for a trial | You pay for a year to learn in a month that it isn't working | The brief's term and notice period |
| Media billed through the agency, or a markup on media, when the brief says you pay media directly | Hidden cost and less visibility into spend | Media paid by you directly; any fee as a flat or stated percentage |
| Fake or bought reviews, followers or engagement; review "seeding" | Breaks platform rules and consumer protection law in most places; can get the listing removed | Remove it; if they want reviews, a process for asking real customers |
| Link schemes, private blog networks, bulk backlinks | Search penalties | Remove it |
| Vague scope: "ongoing optimization", "as needed", "various improvements", "unlimited" | You can't hold them to it, and you can't compare proposals | Quantities, frequency and specs per deliverable, matching the brief's table |
| Unverifiable claims about themselves ("300+ businesses", "proprietary AI outperforms…") | Can't be checked; often recycled | References you can call and examples of similar work |
| Sample copy that breaks the brand rules or uses unapproved claims | A sign they didn't read the brand pack | Revised samples within the rules |
| Fee over budget, or deliverables missing | Doesn't fit the brief | Either the brief's scope at the brief's budget, or a priced option list |
| Subcontracting not disclosed, or no named team | You don't know who does the work | Names and roles; disclosure of any subcontractor |

## Not red flags

Don't flag these, and don't pad the review with them:
- An estimate or range labelled as an estimate, with assumptions or "we don't guarantee results".
- Asking for standard or admin **user access** to accounts the company owns. That's the right way to do it.
- Things the brief didn't ask for, or matters of style in how the proposal is written.
- Out-of-scope items clearly listed as out of scope.
- A detail the brief left open (`TBD`): note it as a question, not a breach.

## The recommendation

| Recommendation | When |
|---|---|
| **Accept** | All deliverables covered, budget and terms fit, no red flags. At most three small clarifications, none blocking |
| **Accept with changes** | Fits overall, but one or two things must change in writing before signing (a missing deliverable, a term, a sample that breaks a rule) |
| **Send back** | Several breaches or one serious red flag, but the agency could fix it: list the changes |
| **Decline** | Red flags that show how they work (fake reviews, guarantees, owning your accounts, asking for passwords) together with a poor fit. Say so plainly, and give the reasons so the user can explain if asked |

If the user likes something that is a red flag ("the guarantee sounds great"), say plainly why it's a problem in one or two sentences, without lecturing, and keep the recommendation where the evidence puts it.

A deadline from the agency ("answer by Friday") is theirs, not the user's. It doesn't change the recommendation; mention it only if it helps ("you can send the changes back before Friday").

## Reviewing a first draft instead of a proposal

Same scorecard, read as: deliverable matches the spec (format, length, quantity), mandatories (voice, rules, claims, accessibility), claims supported, on time, and within the agreed review round. Quote each problem and give the fix in a line. Don't rewrite the draft.
